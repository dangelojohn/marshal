# Stakeholder analysis — who wins, who kills it, and what gets each above 8/10

**Method:** three parallel deep analyses (facility economics · GSC/organiser/EAC ·
exhibitor/carrier/worker/insurer), each scoring stakeholders 1–10 and required to
name the specific change that reaches 8+.

> ⚠ All dollar figures illustrative. No web access, no venue data. **Structural
> findings are well-grounded; every number is a question to ask.**

---

| Stakeholder | Position | Now | Blocking power | Gets to 8+ by |
|---|---|---|---|---|

# ⭐⭐ Finding 1 — the launch sequence was decided for us

> **GSC blocking power: 7/10 at a convention centre. 2/10 at a hotel.**

At a convention centre the GSC is embedded through multi-year official-contractor
agreements and controls the union labour relationship. **At a hotel there is
usually no GSC at all.**

This **independently confirms hotels-first** from a completely different
direction than I'd argued. I said hotels because the physical constraints are
worse, the tooling thinner, and the relationships already exist.

**The real reason is that the party most able to kill Marshal isn't in the
building.**

---

# 🔴 Finding 2 — the workers can kill this silently, and they're the likeliest to

Every other stakeholder's objection is **loud and negotiable**. Exhibitors
complain publicly. Carriers escalate commercially. Insurers simply decline to
credit anything.

**Workers have both motive and *silent* means.** A runner doesn't grieve
anything — they tap sloppily, leave the device in a locker, let the battery die,
or badge-share. **Invisible until the data has been worthless for months.**

### The cascade makes it worse

```
worker distrust → sloppy taps → data quality collapses
   → insurer's evidentiary case is gutted (their value depended on honest data)
   → the last external check on "is the fee buying anything?" disappears
   → the exhibitor pays more for less, and Marshal can never prove otherwise
```

### The objection I have no clean answer to

> *"You tell me it's not tracking me, and you tell the insurance company it
> proves who touched what and when — pick one."*

**That is correct, and it exposes a dishonesty in my own framing.** D-009 says
*"tag equipment, never people."* But operator identity is on every movement —
that's precisely what makes the record valuable to an underwriter. **The data
does identify individuals.**

> ### The honest reformulation, replacing D-009's wording
> **Record the movement, including who performed it. Never track location
> between movements. Never use it for performance management.**
>
> **The commitment is about USE, not existence.** Claiming the data isn't about
> people is a lie a worker will see through in a week, and being caught in it
> costs more than the promise was worth.

### The intervention — and why it must come first

**Put "no individual performance metrics, no discipline use, no leaderboards"
into enforceable collective-agreement language, co-designed with the union
*before* rollout.**

Not announced afterwards as product philosophy. *"A promise workers helped write,
with contractual teeth, is categorically different from one management can
quietly reinterpret in eighteen months."*

This removes the **motive** to defeat the system rather than trying to detect
defeat afterwards — **a detection arms race workers always win, because they
control the physical act of tapping.**

### The certification block is the sharpest edge

If a certification lapses because **the employer's** tracking failed — a common
real-world failure — and the system autonomously benches someone with no human in
the loop, that is algorithmic management at its worst. **Worse than tracking, in
a sense: it has an immediate, public consequence for income and dignity.**

**Red lines** (worth conceding before being asked): any individual-timing use in
discipline · any leaderboard or ranking, in writing · any autonomous *"you cannot
work today"* without **mandatory same-shift human override with zero punitive
consequence.**

### What makes a runner actually glad it exists

1. **It ends radio chaos and double-dispatch** — one clear job, nobody talking
   over each other, no arriving at a truck someone already started
2. **⭐ It becomes their alibi, not their leash** — *use the record publicly to
   back runners against unfair blame the first few times it matters.* The lesson
   lands through experience, never through policy language
3. Gloved, one-handed, no eyes on a screen mid-lift — a real ergonomic win
4. **Cert-gating protects them too** — never pressured to operate outside their
   ticket because a supervisor was short "just this once"

# 🔴 Finding 3 — the exhibitor is three segments, and flat pricing is regressive twice over

| Segment | Detention bill today? | How a dock fee reads |
|---|---|---|
| **Small, self-driven van** | **None** — no carrier invoice exists | **Purely additive.** A new fee for something that was free |
| **Mid/large, contracted carrier** | **Yes** — detention and rush-labour on a delayed, opaque invoice | **Genuine substitution** |
| Advance-warehouse shipper | Insulated by routing | Largely irrelevant |

> **Regressive on two axes at once.** A flat $150 is a rounding error for a 20×20
> island and a real percentage of a 10×10 van exhibitor's total logistics spend —
> **and the van exhibitor is also the one least likely to have a bill being
> replaced.**
>
> **The group most likely to experience it as a junk fee is the group least able
> to point to what it displaced.** That is the worst possible combination
> politically.

**Fixes:**
- **Tier the fee by freight volume / vehicle class.** Flat pricing is indefensible
- **⭐ A contractual backstop: miss your slot by >30 min and we cover documented
  detention / waive the rebooking fee.** This converts the fee from a lottery
  ticket into **real risk transfer** — and it aligns incentives perfectly, since
  Marshal and the venue only keep the fee if the slot is honoured
- **For small exhibitors, lead with show-open certainty, not cost substitution** —
  the substitution claim is *false* for them, and they'll know it

### The sentence for the segment where it's true

> *"You already pay for every hour your truck waits. The only question is whether
> you pay it as a scheduled $150 you control, or an unscheduled $400+
> detention-and-rush-labour bill you don't see coming until the invoice."*

**Honest only for contracted-carrier exhibitors.** Say it to a self-driving
small exhibitor and it reads as false to exactly the audience least able to shrug
off $150.

# Finding 4 — the GSC's real objection is margin, not pride

> *"The exhibitor doesn't do resource accounting. They see two bills for one
> truck and they blame us for the total, not you."*

The chain: exhibitors push back on total freight spend → hand-carry more, ship
less, consolidate → **GSC billable tonnage softens** → organisers use *"exhibitors
are complaining"* as renewal leverage.

**Dangerous because it's unprovable either way.** The anecdote — *"tonnage's been
down since that venue put in the dock fee"* — circulates in GSC leadership long
before anyone has numbers, **and the anecdote alone triggers lobbying.**

> **Counter-move: instrument CWT/tonnage trends at pilot venues from day one.
> Don't let the anecdote get a two-year head start on the evidence.**

**Correction to my earlier recommendation:** I said contractors get a free
read-only feed forever. **For the nationals that's table stakes, not a
concession.** Minimum credible package: **API write-access** (their freight
system stays the operational layer), **co-branding on exhibitor-facing
touchpoints**, and a **modest revenue share funded from Marshal's take, never
their margin**. Not a governance seat — that re-captures the inefficiency Marshal
exists to remove.

**Lead with liability and insurance, not scheduling efficiency.** It maps to
their P&L rather than their pride.

# Finding 5 — two corrections to the financial case

### The damage and insurance story is real but small

| | Dock fee | Everything else | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base case | **$900,000** | $161,500 | ≈$1,061,500 |

**The dock fee is 73–96% of the facility's gain.** Damage deterrence,
attribution, overtime and insurance are a **~18% uplift, not a second pillar.**

**Build the pitch on the dock-fee line — the one high-confidence number. Damage
and insurance are colour, not foundation.**

### Insurance is oversold as premium reduction — and can backfire

Realistic: **$0–10K year one**, rising to $10–25K after 2–3 renewal cycles of
demonstrated loss experience. GL underwriting is dominated by loss history.

**And it cuts both ways:** if the system records near-misses the facility then
fails to act on, plaintiff's counsel gets a *"you had the data and didn't act"*
heightened-duty argument.

**Never lead with "cuts your insurance."** Lead with **"makes your claims cheaper
to fight and easier to win."** And the strongest near-term version: **if the
facility self-insures its GL retention layer, its risk manager benefits from
every avoided incident immediately** — before any carrier's rate table moves.

# Finding 6 — the exhibitor-only-payer model has a governance flaw

Every other party captures benefit at zero marginal cost. **Only the exhibitor
writes a cheque — so nobody but the exhibitor has skin in the game to keep the
fee honest.**

A venue under revenue pressure has every incentive to let it creep. **Marshal's
own commercial incentive points at the stakeholder least able to push back.**

**Self-imposed discipline is the only check:** publish evidence of what the fee
prevents · tier and cap it · offer a credible remedy (the detention backstop) ·
**contractually cap Marshal's take with no unilateral escalation.**

# The take rate — final recommendation

| take | Marshal | facility keeps | breakeven venues |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | $45K | $855K | 10.7 |
| **18%** | **$162K** | **$738K** | **2.6** |
| 25% | $225K | $675K | 1.8 |

**Recommendation: 18%, with two structural additions.**

1. **A per-booking floor of $15–20** — without it, our own recommendation to
   discount off-peak slots (which flattens the peak) would erode the take. I had
   missed this entirely
2. **Volume decay to ~15% above ~$1.5M/yr** at a venue — the largest venues keep
   getting an obviously good deal as they scale

**Thresholds:** ≤20–22% reads as an obvious yes against anchors a GM already
knows (exclusive-contractor commissions back to venues commonly run 10–20%).
**~25%** invites *"why am I giving away a quarter of a revenue line on my own
dock, forever, to a vendor I could replace?"* **>30–35%** reads as a landlord on
the facility's own asset.

**Correction to my own reasoning:** I cited hotel OTAs (15–25%) as support.
**Wrong comparable** — OTA commission is priced substantially for *bringing the
customer*, and Marshal brings no exhibitors; the trucks are coming regardless.
The right anchor is **a fair split of newly created value for real operational
work**, which is parking-reservation and ticketing-service-fee territory.

**No cut of the damage-savings bucket** — it's an avoided cost, not a
transaction, impossible to audit (*"prove it would have happened without you"*),
and reads as scope creep. A contingency fee on *recovered* damage is precedented
and defensible, **but keep it out of the core model** — it sits in tension with
our own rule that Marshal never profits when the floor goes wrong.
