## 0. The single most important fact this pass turned up, found by accident

**GES no longer belongs to a diversified travel/hospitality conglomerate — it was sold off as a standalone business to a private equity firm, and its former parent renamed itself to make the separation explicit.** [SOURCED — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viad_Corp, cross-checked against SEC filing index search results]

Viad Corp owned two operating businesses: GES (event/exhibition services) and "Pursuit" (hospitality/attractions — lodges, restaurants, transportation at Banff, Jasper, Glacier National Park, etc.), formalized as two named segments starting in 2015. On **October 21, 2024**, Viad signed a definitive agreement to sell GES to **Truelink Capital**, a private equity firm; the deal closed **December 31, 2024**. Viad Corp then renamed itself **Pursuit Attractions and Hospitality, Inc.** (NYSE: PRSU) and now describes itself as a "pure-play attractions and hospitality business." [SOURCED — same]

Why this matters for the JTBD question specifically: this is not marketing-page framing, it's a **boardroom-and-M&A-market judgment**, made by the people with the most information and the strongest financial incentive to get the categorization right. When forced to choose, Viad's own leadership decided GES did **not** belong bundled with a hospitality/travel/leisure business — it was sold to a buyer who values it as a standalone logistics-and-labor operating company. A PE firm doesn't buy "experiential marketing magic"; it buys cash flow it understands well enough to underwrite debt against. That a financial buyer was willing to do that for GES specifically, separated cleanly from hospitality, is about as concrete a real-world confirmation of "this is a different kind of business than its marketing suggests" as you can get — and it is exactly the kind of fact a vendor-marketing-page research pass (file 04) would never surface, because it isn't on any vendor's marketing page. See §6 for how this changes file 04's framing slightly (GES is now PE-owned, not Viad-owned — worth a note back in that file if it's ever refreshed, flagged here rather than edited there per this task's no-code/no-other-files instruction).

One important caveat found in the same research thread: **"GES" is also the initialism used internally by Gartner for "Global Exhibitor Sales"** — a completely unrelated sales-team brand name at the research/advisory firm Gartner, which produces its own "Account Executive, Exhibitor Sales" job postings that have nothing to do with the trade-show GES. [SOURCED — jobs.gartner.com postings surfaced under a GES search] Flagging this explicitly because it's a real name collision that could contaminate future research if not caught — none of the Gartner postings are used anywhere below.

## 1. Reconstructing move-in day — what evidence actually supports, and what it doesn't

**Honesty check first:** no source found this session gives a literal clock-time ("6:00 AM: ... 7:00 AM: ...") breakdown of a GSC move-in day. I looked for that specifically (trade-press "day in the life" profiles) and did not find one — see §5 for the full list of what didn't turn up. What follows is a sequence reconstructed from job postings, employee reviews, and GSC/union process descriptions, each piece individually [SOURCED] to what it's built from, with the *sequencing and stitching-together* marked [SPECULATIVE]. Treat it as directionally right, not literally transcribed from any single document.

**Before the dock opens.** Advance-warehouse freight (already sitting in a GSC warehouse from the pre-show advance-rate window — see file 04 §5) gets staged for the move-in date. CDL drivers running the GSC's own box trucks move freight from warehouse to show site: one posting describes drivers "driving 26' box trucks from warehouse to show site, assisting with unloading and setting up trade show floors, and remaining onsite to perform daily duties as assigned by the onsite Operations Manager" [SOURCED — Indeed/CDL trade-show driver posting language, surfaced via search synthesis]. A Freeman "Class C Driver" reviewer, Austin TX, on Indeed: *"The warehouse is huge and can get hectic"* and *"I delivered equipment to show sites and felt a pride representing Freeman"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Freeman-49d883ed/reviews].

**Dock opens, freight starts moving continuously.** A GES forklift operator review on Indeed describes the actual task loop in near-literal terms: *"Be 15 minutes before time to start a lot of forklift driving to the dock from the dock unloading trucks loading trucks... Work together work safe work steady and get to work on time always"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Ges-Exposition-Services/reviews?fjobtitle=Forklift+Operator]. This is a continuous truck-to-dock-to-floor-and-back cycle, not a one-time unload — it runs all day as trucks queue.

**Concurrently, on the floor, union craft labor builds the show.** Per GES's own labor-management content (already partially covered structurally in file 04 §6, but this is the task-level detail): install/dismantle crews build exhibits, signage, and floor coverings; electrical labor installs lighting/AV/electronic displays; plumbing labor handles water/gas/air connections; forklift operators (certified) move heavy items; rigging professionals install hanging signs; equipment/lift operators and scissor-lift operators handle bulky items and high/hard-to-reach work [SOURCED — insights.ges.com/us-exhibitor-roadmap/trade-show-labor]. A Freeman "Stage Hand" reviewer in Orlando described the work itself as *"physically demanding mathematical experienced & blueprint reading"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Freeman-49d883ed/reviews] — i.e., the craft-labor floor work involves reading construction blueprints and doing on-the-fly measurement/math, not just carrying boxes.

**A supervisor is moving between the branch office and the floor all day, not sitting at a desk.** Freeman's own "Supervisor Operations" job description (Orlando and Nashville postings) frames the role as *"leading, supervising, and supporting the operations team at show site and in the Branch Office while keeping job costs at the lowest possible level and maintaining quality and safety standards,"* including directly supervising/training/disciplining crew, interacting with customers and third-party vendors, and *"promoting and maintaining a strong safety culture."* [SOURCED — glassdoor.com Freeman "Supervisor Operations" / "Operations Supervisor" postings, Orlando FL and Nashville TN, via search synthesis] The hard requirements for this role are the tell: **two years of supervisory experience AND two years of show-site freight, decorating, and/or I&D experience, carpentry skills required, OSHA 30 Certification required** [SOURCED — same postings]. This is a construction-foreman hiring bar with an events title on it, not an events-management or hospitality hiring bar.

**Meanwhile, exhibitor-facing staff are fielding a different kind of fire.** A Shepard employee review describes the exhibitor-services side of the job as *"answering phones, being in chats and answering emails with customers, prepping shows, working at show site"* [SOURCED — glassdoor.com Shepard Exposition Services reviews, via search synthesis]. Freeman's own "Exhibitor Services Specialist" posting (Dallas, TX, closed role) spells this out at task level: *"Enter orders, enter exhibitor information, research invoices, and research show information in Freeman billing systems"*; *"Process, research, and resolve complex requirements, inquiries, and complaints to ensure customer retention"*; manage inbound leads and quote pricing within policy; *"proactively identifies, researches, and resolves customer order discrepancies"*; and — notably — *"prepare on-site teams for account changes and customer needs,"* meaning this desk-bound order-entry/billing role is in the loop that feeds the floor crew what to actually do. [SOURCED — career.com Freeman Exhibitor Services Specialist posting, $42,106–$53,544/yr, Dallas TX]

**All day, the clock is running against union jurisdiction and deadline rules.** Every task above is filtered through whichever venue's specific labor-jurisdiction rulebook applies (McCormick Place's time/tool-based carve-outs vs. Javits' size-based carve-outs — both already fully documented in file 04 §6). Nothing in this session's search added new venues to that picture; it just confirmed, from the labor side rather than the venue side, that GES's own "Trade Show Labor Communication and Leadership Strategies" content advises exhibitors to schedule labor during weekday business hours specifically to avoid overtime rates and to consult the show's specific exhibitor service manual — i.e. even GES's own advice to *exhibitors* is "know your jurisdiction's overtime clock or it will cost you," which tells you the same clock governs the GSC's own crew costs internally. [SOURCED — insights.ges.com/us-exhibitor-roadmap/trade-show-labor]

**Deadlines compound into stacked shows, not single events.** Multiple independent reviews across GES, Fern, and Shepard converge on the same structural fact: shows run back-to-back during peak season with minimal gap. GES: *"Tight deadlines during peak seasons, mostly summer and early fall,"* *"sometimes there are no days off because trade shows happen back to back"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Ges-Exposition-Services/reviews]. Fern: *"The only time it is real stressful is when you have 4+ shows going on"* (Warehouse Associate) [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Fern-Expo/reviews]. Shepard: *"Be prepared to have to do the work of multiple positions with the pay of only one position"* during busy stretches [SOURCED — glassdoor.com Shepard reviews via search synthesis].

**Net reconstruction (marked SPECULATIVE for the sequencing/stitching, each component above SOURCED):** a move-in day is (1) pre-dawn/early staging of advance-warehouse freight by drivers and warehouse crew, (2) a continuous truck-dock-floor forklift cycle running the full shift, (3) parallel union-craft-labor construction of the floor (carpentry, electrical, rigging, plumbing) governed by that specific venue's jurisdiction rules and running against an overtime clock, (4) an Operations Supervisor physically circulating between branch office and floor doing safety enforcement, cost tracking, and crew direction, and (5) a desk-based exhibitor-services layer simultaneously fielding phone/chat/email order and billing problems from exhibitors and feeding urgent changes back to the floor. Overtime and weekend work are described as routine, not exceptional, by reviewers at all three companies checked (Freeman, GES, Fern, Shepard) — this is a chronic operating condition of the business, not a seasonal edge case.

## 2. The 2-4 core competencies, ranked by what the evidence actually shows about headcount vs. margin concentration

This ranking synthesizes today's labor-side evidence with file 04's already-sourced pricing/margin facts (specifically file 04 §5's "material handling is the most-complained-about, most profitable line" and §8's "labor is pass-through-plus-markup"). I am not re-deriving those facts — citing them as established context — and layering today's headcount/task evidence on top.

**1. Warehouse/dock/forklift logistics execution — highest headcount, and (per file 04) the highest-margin revenue line, which is a genuinely important and non-obvious pairing.**
This is not two separate things — the same blunt physical competency (moving freight from truck to dock to floor and back, on a certified-weight basis) is *both* the largest labor pool *and* the line file 04 already identified as the GSC's most profitable [INDUSTRY-COMMON per file 04 §5/§8]. That's structurally different from a software or brand-licensing business where the money and the headcount concentrate in different places. Evidence for headcount concentration here: the volume and specificity of forklift-operator, warehouse-associate, and CDL-driver reviews and postings found this session dwarfed every other job family searched — multiple independent postings and reviews, consistent task language, consistent complaints (long hours, "hectic," contingent hiring — see below) [SOURCED, aggregated from the review/posting fetches in §1].

**2. Union/craft-labor jurisdiction management — a supervisory competency the whole operation is gated on, priced pass-through-plus-markup, so its *margin* per hour is thin (per file 04 §8) but its *risk* is existential — get a jurisdiction call wrong and the venue can stop work on the floor.**
Evidence: Freeman's Operations Supervisor hiring bar (2 yrs supervisory + 2 yrs show-site freight/decorating/I&D, carpentry skills, OSHA 30) [SOURCED, §1] and the venue-specific jurisdiction rulebooks already fully documented in file 04 §6 (McCormick Place's do/don't list, Javits' two-man-plus-forklift rigging crews and size-based self-install carve-out). This is the competency that makes the floor legally and physically buildable at all — but per file 04 §8, it's billed pass-through-plus-markup on union hourly rates, not a high-margin product line the way drayage is.

**3. Contingent/seasonal workforce scaling — an under-marketed competency that showed up repeatedly and unprompted in employee reviews, not in any job posting or vendor page, and it deserves its own line because it's a genuinely distinct operational skill from "logistics."**
Evidence, independently from two different companies: a GES Glassdoor reviewer stated the company *"only hire[s] temp and you have to work hard to become permanent, as they hire around the busy season to staff their shows and then fire everyone"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/GES search synthesis]. A Fern Expo Warehouse Supervisor reviewer, describing hiring practice directly: *"Uses temporary employees and hire very few [permanent]"* [SOURCED — indeed.com/cmp/Fern-Expo/reviews]. The same Fern reviewer, unprompted, added: *"Works with felons and I love that about the company"* [SOURCED — same] — evidence the labor pool being drawn from is one that competes on availability/flexibility rather than credential scarcity, consistent with a staffing-agency-style hire-fast/release-fast model layered underneath the union-jurisdiction structure in competency #2. This is a materially different management competency than "coordinate a fleet" or "run a warehouse" — it's "stand up and tear down a workforce on a weekly cadence, city to city, without breaking safety/quality standards or violating union agreements" — and no vendor marketing page uses this language about itself, for obvious reputational reasons.

**4. Exhibitor-facing sales/order administration — smallest headcount footprint of the four found this session, and the one most insulated from the physical grind, but structurally the highest-leverage per person:** it is a retail-transaction/account-management competency (order entry, invoice research, complaint resolution, inbound-lead quoting) layered on top of the physical operation, evidenced by Freeman's Exhibitor Services Specialist posting and Shepard reviews describing the same desk-bound order/billing workflow [SOURCED, §1]. This is closest to the user's "carpet selling company" framing — a transactional retail/rental sale, order-taking and invoicing, sitting on top of an industrial logistics-and-labor operation underneath it. I could not find revenue-per-headcount data to rank this quantitatively against #1-3 — flagged as [SPECULATIVE] ranking based on task-description insulation from physical labor, not a sourced margin figure.

**Ranking summary, headcount (highest to lowest), based on volume/specificity of evidence found this session:** warehouse/dock/forklift > union craft labor supervision > contingent-workforce management (a management overlay on #1, not separately staffed) > exhibitor services/sales. **Ranking summary, margin concentration (per file 04's already-sourced facts, not re-derived here):** material handling/drayage (executed by competency #1) is explicitly the highest-margin line; labor (competency #2) is pass-through-plus-markup, thinner margin per hour; sales/account competency (#4) margin unknown/unsourced this session.

## 3. Five JTBD statements, grounded in the specific evidence above

**1.** When move-in day dock traffic backs up and trucks queue outside faster than pallets can be staged, a **forklift operator/warehouse crew member** wants to keep the truck-to-dock-to-floor cycle moving without a gap, so they can hit the shift's throughput target without turning a 12-hour day into an unpaid-overtime dispute. *(Grounded in: "Be 15 minutes before time to start... unloading trucks loading trucks... work steady," GES/Fern reviews on chronic long hours and back-to-back shows.)*

**2.** When peak season stacks 4+ shows in overlapping weeks, an **Operations/Warehouse Supervisor** wants a crew that can flex up fast via temporary/contingent hires without breaking safety standards or union agreements, so they can staff every floor on the calendar without carrying full-time headcount through the slow season. *(Grounded in: Fern "Uses temporary employees and hire very few," GES "hire around the busy season... then fire everyone," Fern "the only time it is real stressful is when you have 4+ shows going on.")*

**3.** When an exhibitor calls in the night before move-in confused about a missing material-handling order or a billing discrepancy, an **Exhibitor Services Representative** wants to resolve it directly — research the invoice, fix the order, requote if needed — without escalating it up the chain, so they can protect the account relationship and the on-site invoice before the floor opens the next morning. *(Grounded in: Freeman Exhibitor Services Specialist posting — "enter orders... research invoices... resolve complex requirements, inquiries, and complaints to ensure customer retention"; Shepard reviews — "answering phones, being in chats and answering emails with customers, prepping shows.")*

**4.** When a booth calls for overhead signage in a venue with strict union jurisdiction, a **Rigging/Operations Supervisor** wants a correctly-sized, correctly-scheduled craft crew (e.g., the two-person-plus-forklift rigging model already documented at Javits in file 04) working under that specific venue's jurisdiction rules, so they can get the sign hung safely without triggering a jurisdictional dispute or an OSHA violation that halts work on the floor. *(Grounded in: Freeman Operations Supervisor's hard requirements — OSHA 30 certification, carpentry skills, 2 years I&D experience — plus file 04's already-sourced Javits/McCormick Place jurisdiction detail.)*

**5.** When staffing a new Operations Supervisor role, a **branch/regional manager** wants someone who already has hands-on carpentry, decorating, or I&D floor experience — not a pure people-management or hospitality background — so they can trust that person to make real-time floor judgment calls (crew safety, task sequencing, customer escalation, cost control) without additional training. *(Grounded directly in Freeman's stated hiring bar: "two years of supervisory experience and two years of show site freight, decorating and/or I&D experience... Carpentry skills required. OSHA 30 Certification required.")*

## 4. The single most surprising / non-obvious finding

**It's the Viad/GES divestiture from §0, and here's why it beats the other strong candidate (the contingent-labor staffing-agency model from §2/#3).**

The contingent-labor finding is genuinely surprising and directly useful — it says the real competency isn't just "move freight," it's "stand up and tear down a blue-collar workforce on a weekly, city-hopping cadence" — but it's still a *labor-practice* finding, the kind you'd eventually infer just from reading enough Glassdoor reviews.

The Viad/Truelink divestiture is a different order of evidence: it's a **capital-markets judgment**, made by people with full financial visibility into GES's actual unit economics (not marketing copy, not employee sentiment — audited numbers and buyer diligence), that GES's business is *not* the same kind of business as hospitality/attractions, and is worth more separated out and run on its own by a private-equity operator than kept bundled inside a "travel and leisure" conglomerate story. **No vendor marketing page would ever surface this** — it's the opposite of what a marketing page is for. A marketing page wants exhibitors and show organizers to believe GES is a seamless "experience" partner; an M&A transaction is the one place the underlying financial reality of the business gets stated plainly enough for outsiders to see, because a buyer had to underwrite real cash flows to do the deal. The fact that this happened at the end of 2024 — recent, not historical — and file 04's own vendor research (done fresh this week) never surfaced it, is itself evidence of exactly the gap this task set out to close: marketing-page research and financial/organizational reality tell two different stories about what these companies actually are.

Practical implication for Marshal, stated as [SPECULATIVE]: if GES is now owned by a PE firm rather than a public parent with a stable multi-decade "keep everything running quietly" incentive, that ownership change could mean either (a) more pressure to modernize/cut costs — including on the "decade-scale technology refresh cycle" file 04 already flagged as a weakness — or (b) more pressure to extract cash and defer capex, making the tech-debt gap worse. This session did not find evidence to distinguish between those two, and it's worth a dedicated follow-up (Truelink Capital's stated investment thesis for GES, if published, would resolve it) rather than guessing further here.
