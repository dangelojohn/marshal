# Exhibitor↔Facility Direct Interaction Map — testing Marshal's dock thesis

**Research date:** 2026-08-07
**Method:** WebSearch + WebFetch, this session. Venue-published exhibitor/move-in
manuals, fire-marshal/AHJ booth-inspection guidance, security/badging procedures,
trade press and news coverage of dock/marshalling-yard incidents, GSC
service-recovery/complaint escalation content, and venue-exclusive utility
ordering pages. Deliberately not re-covering GSC/venue *software* marketing —
that's files 01-08. Not re-covering GSC or venue *labor JTBD* — that's files 09
and 10, read first as inputs (see "Relationship to files 09/10" below).

**Relationship to files 09 and 10:** file 09 reconstructed the GSC crew's day;
file 10 reconstructed the venue ops staff's day and already found the dock
coordinator role, McCormick Place's four-wheel-dolly rule, and the OSHA
search-tool dead end. This file does not repeat that ground. Its question is
narrower and specifically adversarial to Marshal's own founding claim: **strip
out every interaction that has a GSC or organizer rep standing in the middle,
and map only what's left** — the moments an exhibitor (or their driver/labor)
deals with venue/facility staff directly, unmediated.

**Tagging key** (same discipline as files 09/10): [SOURCED] = confirmed via a
live fetch/search today, URL cited. [VENDOR] = venue/GSC's own marketing/manual
copy — usually accurate about *procedure* but silent about *friction*.
[INDUSTRY-COMMON] = standard knowledge, not independently re-verified today.
[UNVERIFIED] = plausible, not confirmed this session. [ANECDOTAL] = a single
first-person account, not corroborated. [SPECULATIVE] = my synthesis/inference,
flagged as such, not a sourced claim.

---

## 0. The map, at a glance

Six angles researched. Two (dock/marshalling yard, fire-marshal/AHJ
inspection) turned up strong, multi-venue, primary-source evidence of real,
unmediated, sometimes high-friction exhibitor-facility contact. Two
(security/badges, venue-exclusive utility install) turned up real but
structurally messier evidence — genuine direct contact, but complicated by
who's actually on the other side of it. One (safety incidents) turned up a
single real, named, monetarily-consequential case, better than file 10's
dead-end but still a thin sample. One (complaint escalation) turned up
almost nothing at the individual level — itself a finding, not just a gap.
Full assembled comparison is §7; the direct thesis verdict is §8.

## 1. The dock/marshalling yard itself

**This is the one angle with a real, primary-source, step-by-step procedure
document, and it confirms direct unmediated contact at multiple steps.**

**Metro Toronto Convention Centre's own "Vehicle Marshalling / Move-In /
Move-Out" bulletin** [SOURCED, mtccc.com/images/content/file/exhibitors/Vehicle_Marshalling.pdf,
dated December 2015 — note: a 2026 GES exhibitor prospectus snippet says "the
Marshalling Yard on Cherry Street is no longer in use for exhibitor move-in,"
superseded by the Voyage Control app per file 07's already-sourced finding, so
treat the *procedure structure* below as historically accurate and
representative, not necessarily MTCC's live 2026 workflow] lays out, in
numbered steps, exactly the moments this research is looking for:

1. All vehicles report first to the marshalling yard (3.8 km from the venue itself).
2. **"An attendant will be posted at the marshalling area. The attendant will
   assign the delivery order of the vehicles and give a numbered ticket to the
   driver"** — the attendant works from "a prearranged priority listing
   produced by show management" but the ticket-issuing interaction itself is
   driver-to-attendant, face to face, no GSC rep present. **"The cost of the
   attendant will be at the expense of the Convention Centre"** during normal
   hours — i.e. this is a venue employee/contractor, not a show-management or
   GSC employee. [SOURCED, same PDF]
3. A call is made to the attendant to dispatch vehicles as dock space frees up.
4. **"Vehicles entering the loading dock areas will be met by the Convention
   Centre dock staff who will control the flow of vehicle traffic."** [SOURCED,
   same] — a second, distinct direct-contact moment: dock staff physically
   meet the truck.
5. **"Vehicles going up onto the North Building Exhibit Floor will be met by
   dock staff positioned at John Street and on the ramp to control the
   pedestrian traffic on the ramp."** [SOURCED, same] — a third distinct
   moment, this time controlling foot traffic, not vehicle flow.
6. **"Freight elevators, when in use, will be manned and controlled by
   operators supplied by the Convention Centre."** [SOURCED, same] — a fourth
   moment: anyone using a freight elevator is directly supervised by a venue
   employee, not a GSC or organizer rep.
7. Vehicle storage during the event is arranged directly "through the Dock
   Office" by phone — a fifth direct-contact channel, this one initiated by
   the exhibitor/driver rather than the venue. [SOURCED, same]

Two things this document makes explicit that matter for Marshal's thesis:
first, **the venue bears the labour cost of the attendant during normal hours**
— it's paying to staff its own side of this interaction, which is a real
signal the venue treats the yard as its own operational responsibility, not
something delegated to a GSC. Second, **show management sets the priority
order, but a Convention Centre employee executes it at the point of contact**
— the queueing *policy* is organizer-controlled, but the *interaction* itself
(ticket issued to a driver's hand, a vehicle physically met and waved through)
is venue-to-driver, with nobody from the GSC or show office standing between
them at that moment. This is close to the cleanest confirmation found in this
whole pass of Marshal's specific claim about the dock.

**Javits Center's own move-in procedures for a client show (JA New York Fall
2025)** [SOURCED, ja-newyork.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Move-In-Procedures.pdf]
independently confirm the same pattern at a completely different venue,
across *two* separate direct-contact channels within the dock/marshalling
system, not just one:

- **Truck deliveries route through "the new truck marshaling facility"** at a
  separate address (369 12th Ave) before reaching the dock. [SOURCED, same]
- **Complimentary porter/labor service is claimed in person at a "Labor Desk,"
  after "follow[ing] the Public Safety officers' directions to park in the
  Inner Roadway"** — i.e. a venue security/public-safety officer physically
  directs the exhibitor's parking, a direct, unmediated instruction, before
  the exhibitor even reaches the labor desk. **Peak-time wait for this
  service runs 25-30 minutes** [SOURCED, same] — a concrete, sourced friction
  point, not a smooth non-event.
- **Self-unloading via POV is capped by policy** ("nothing larger than a
  two-wheeled baggage cart," "hand carts & dollies are not permitted," "one
  person must remain with the vehicle at ALL times," "vehicle must then be
  moved") [SOURCED, same] — this mirrors file 10's McCormick Place
  four-wheel-dolly finding exactly, at a second venue, strengthening the
  earlier file's claim that these limits are jurisdictional/enforcement rules
  applied at the literal curb, not abstract policy language.

**What the attendant/dock-staff role does NOT appear to control, per this
document:** the *priority order* (organizer's call), the *content* of what's
in the truck (GSC/EAC's business), or anything about booth setup once freight
is on the floor. Its authority is narrowly: sequencing physical access to a
scarce resource (dock doors, freight elevators, ramp) and enforcing physical
safety/traffic rules at the point of contact. That's a small, sharply bounded
authority — but it is authority nobody else in the chain holds, and it is
exercised in a face-to-face interaction with the driver, not through a portal.

**What happens with no reservation, specifically — closed, not speculative.**
A live 2025 exhibitor-facing document for a show at MTCC states it explicitly:
**"You must register to gain access to the LOADING DOCKS for your setup. No
guaranteed access without an appointment. Should you fail to register in
advance you will need to wait until a time slot or space in the loading area
becomes available."** [SOURCED, Toronto Comicon 2025 Operating Guidelines,
fanexpohq.com/uploads/TCC25-OPERATING-GUIDELINES..., §"Voyage Control," see
§3 below for the rest of this document] This confirms the mechanism inferred
above almost exactly: an unscheduled truck isn't turned away, it's held until
capacity frees up — the same physical queue the 2015 paper-ticket system
describes, now software-mediated through Voyage Control. **The same document
also confirms MTCC still physically operates a marshalling yard as a fallback
path**, independent of Voyage Control: MTCC's own current (2024-25) Fire
Regulations for Exhibitors state **"All exhibitors are to report to the
Marshaling Yard prior to Move-In or Move-Out, except shows using Voyage
Control for scheduled Move-In"** [SOURCED, mtccc.com Fire Regulations for
Exhibitors 2024-25, full text captured in §2] — i.e. the 2015 procedure isn't
dead, it's the non-software-mediated branch for shows that haven't adopted
Voyage Control, which corrects file 07/09's implication that the marshalling
yard itself is defunct; only its role as the *default* path has been
partially displaced.

## 2. Fire marshal / life-safety booth inspection

**This is the single strongest counter-evidence found in this whole pass
against Marshal's thesis being about the *only* unmediated touchpoint.** Every
venue-published fire-safety document found this session — three, from two
different cities, in two different countries — independently describes the
same structure: a two-tier authority, both tiers **outside the GSC/organizer
chain entirely**, with real, no-recourse power over an exhibitor's booth.

**Tier 1 — a venue employee, contacted directly by the exhibitor, pre-show.**
MTCC's fire-safety compliance function is a named venue role — the **"Safety
Compliance Manager of the Metro Toronto Convention Centre"** — with a direct
phone line and, per a live 2026-27 show manual, **a direct venue email
(FireSafetyReply@mtccc.com) that exhibitors are told explicitly to use
without going through the show office**: *"The form will be submitted
directly to the venue fire marshal; you do not need to email [the organizer]"*
[SOURCED, nationalwomenshow.com/exhibitor-manuals/toronto-exhibitor-manual/].
McCormick Place's equivalent is the **"Fire Safety Manager"**, named with a
direct phone number and email (312-791-6079, dcozzi@mccormickplace.com)
[SOURCED, wef.org McCormick Place Fire Safety Regulations PDF]. This tier
handles written approvals (propane equipment, tents, motorized vehicles,
booth floor plans over a size threshold) — an administrative, mostly
pre-show, mostly asynchronous channel, but **a direct one**: the exhibitor
emails or calls the venue's own safety office, not the GSC, not the organizer
(the organizer/Show Manager is sometimes the *conduit* for the paperwork —
MTCC's process for special-approval materials routes through "the Show
Manager" who then submits to the Safety Compliance Manager — but the venue's
current live version bypasses even that: the National Women's Show manual
explicitly tells exhibitors to skip the email-to-organizer step).

**Tier 2 — an external city/AHJ inspector, physically walking the floor,
with real-time shutdown authority.** This is the tier that matters most for
testing the thesis, because it is (a) unambiguously outside the GSC/organizer
chain — a Fire Marshal is a municipal fire-department employee or an
Authority Having Jurisdiction, not a venue or show-management staffer — and
(b) documented, at every venue checked, as a physical, in-person, pre-open
inspection with the power to prohibit a booth from exhibiting:

- **MTCC**: "Fire prevention... will be strictly enforced by the Safety
  Compliance Manager of the Metro Toronto Convention Centre **and the
  Toronto Fire Prevention Division**" [SOURCED, MTCC Fire Regulations for
  Exhibitors 2024-25 — full text captured this session]. A separate,
  organizer-facing show manual for a different MTCC event warns exhibitors
  directly: *"If you do not get approval, you can risk being shut down before
  the show opens when the fire marshal conducts their inspection"* [SOURCED,
  nationalwomenshow.com, as above].
- **McCormick Place**: *"Fire Prevention reserves the right to make any
  final decisions... according to Section 2-36-220 of the Municipal Code of
  Chicago on Fire Prevention. Prior to the show opening and at any time
  during the event, the Fire Marshal may inspect the booths... if a booth
  imposes a significant fire hazard, it will be prohibited from exhibiting"*
  [SOURCED, wef.org McCormick Place Fire Safety Regulations PDF]. The same
  document elsewhere separately names the **Chicago Fire Department** as the
  pre-approval authority for pyrotechnics/lasers/open flame — the Fire
  Marshal doing the floor walk and the Fire Department reviewing hazard
  submissions read as the same municipal chain, distinct from both
  McCormick Place's own Fire Safety Manager and from any GSC.
- **A live 2025 operating-guidelines document (Toronto Comicon at MTCC)**
  confirms the same authority is exercised on granular, individual physical
  objects, not just the floor plan as a whole, and **that exhibitors are
  billed directly by the venue for it**: *"All vehicles must abide by the
  move-in and move-out schedules... and are subject to inspection by the
  Fire Marshall prior to move in on the floor. **Exhibitors will be billed
  for such inspections.**"* [SOURCED, fanexpohq.com TCC25 Operating
  Guidelines §8.10]. The same document separately states floor plans
  submitted late "may be disallowed by the Fire Marshall with no recourse"
  [SOURCED, same, §8.4] — language notably more absolute ("no recourse")
  than anything found describing GSC or organizer authority in this
  document.

**A first-hand anecdote confirms this tier is not a paperwork formality —
it happens live, close to doors-open, and can force a real-time physical
change.** A trade-show consultant's account, posted to LinkedIn: *"That call
came from the company president 45 minutes before an international trade
show was to open... 'The fire marshal refuses to open the show until we
remove the canopy from the booth.'"* The booth's canopy design had been
pre-approved and used successfully the prior year at the same venue; the
consultant's own read is that a "trades director" she'd been short with
during install may have flagged the booth to the fire marshal — i.e. **even
the trigger for a fire-marshal escalation can run through a labor/trades
relationship**, not just an objective code check. [ANECDOTAL — single
first-hand account, not corroborated, via
linkedin.com/posts/pweigel_tradeshows...] Directionally consistent with, and
independently corroborating, the sourced "no recourse"/"prohibited from
exhibiting" language above.

**Why this specifically complicates Marshal's thesis, not just adds a data
point:** the README's claim is that the dock is "the only part of an
exhibition floor that no service-exclusivity contract covers." That's a
claim about *commercial exclusivity* — and on that narrow reading it may
still be defensible (see §8). But if the operative question is "where does
an exhibitor deal directly with a facility/authority figure, no GSC or
organizer in the room," fire-safety inspection clears that bar at least as
cleanly as the dock does, at every venue checked, and with a materially
higher single-event consequence (total exclusion from the show, not a
delayed truck). It is also **not a scheduling/allocation problem the way the
dock is** — see §8 for why that distinction, not "is it direct," is probably
the more useful cut for evaluating Marshal's wedge.

**One more government inspector surfaced, previously undocumented in this
corpus:** MTCC's fire regulations name a *third* independent authority with
floor-level power over exhibitor equipment — Ontario's **Electrical Safety
Authority (ESA)**: *"Electrical Safety Authority (ESA) inspectors have the
authority to order the removal of unapproved electrical equipment from the
show"* [SOURCED, MTCC Fire Regulations for Exhibitors 2024-25, §"Electrical
equipment and connections"]. Any electrical equipment lacking an approved
certification mark requires the exhibitor to file directly with ESA (not the
venue, not the GSC) for a "Permission to Show Unapproved Electrical
Equipment" waiver. This is a second, distinct AHJ-level direct-interaction
channel this task's brief didn't anticipate — flagged here because it's real
and sourced, not because it changes the overall map materially; it's
structurally the same kind of thing as the Fire Marshal (external government
inspector, floor authority, no GSC/organizer intermediary).

## 3. Security / badge checkpoints

**Real, but the messiest angle of the six — because "security" turns out to
be a mixed category, not cleanly venue or cleanly organizer, and the evidence
this session found actually argues both ways.**

**Clean venue-direct example — Javits.** The move-in procedure quoted in §1
has an unambiguous venue-security moment: *"follow the Public Safety
officers' directions to park in the Inner Roadway in front of the Javits
Center, and then go to the Labor Desk"* [SOURCED, ja-newyork.com Move-In
Procedures PDF]. Javits' Public Safety function is the building's own —
distinct from the show's GSC or organizer — and it physically directs
vehicle/pedestrian flow before an exhibitor even reaches a service desk.

**Ambiguous example — MTCC, across two documents that don't agree on who's
staffing the door.** MTCC's own 2024-25 Fire Regulations state plainly:
*"Exhibitors will be issued a pass to receive authorization to enter the
loading area"* [SOURCED, as in §1/§2] — this reads as a venue-issued
credential, separate from the show's own exhibitor badge. But a live 2025
organizer manual for a show *at* MTCC describes the daily hall-entrance
checkpoint differently: *"Exhibitors may enter the hall through the loading
dock or freight elevators from the North Garage ONLY during scheduled
move-in or restocking times. The Exhibit Hall entrance will be staffed by
security guards, be prepared to show your exhibitor badge"* [SOURCED, TCC25
Operating Guidelines §4.4] — and the same document's own Section 20
("Additional Security") clarifies that *extra* security coverage is
organizer-procured, not the venue's: *"Show Management will provide at least
one security guard overnight... Exhibitor must arrange for any desired extra
security through Show Management and must hire from our designated security
company"* [SOURCED, same, §20.1-20.2]. **This is a genuine, sourced
complication, not a gap in the research**: at an organizer-run consumer show
inside a venue, at least some of the uniformed people checking badges at the
hall door are contracted *by the organizer*, not venue employees — meaning
not every "direct interaction with a person in a security-guard role" is
actually a direct *venue* interaction. The task's angle 3 asked specifically
about interaction with *venue* (not GSC/organizer) security — and the honest
finding is that this line is blurrier in practice than the brief assumed,
at least at large third-party-organizer shows. It's cleanest at venues like
Javits where "Public Safety" is legibly a venue department; it's murkier at
a venue like MTCC hosting an organizer-run consumer show, where "security"
in the exhibitor manual is at least partly a Show-Management vendor.

**Damage/condition inspection is a separate, clean venue-direct security-
adjacent touchpoint.** The Comicon operating guidelines state: *"Upon
removal of each booth, Facility and Show Management will inspect each space
for any damages incurred by the exhibitor... Any charges to make good the
exhibit space will be passed on to the exhibitor"* [SOURCED, TCC25 Operating
Guidelines, "Damage Inspection"]. "Facility" here is explicitly named
alongside, not merged into, "Show Management" — a second confirmation
(after the Colorado CC Dock/Conveyance Coordinator's pre/post-event walk
already sourced in file 10) that a venue employee, not a GSC or organizer
rep, personally inspects and bills for booth-space condition.

**Net for this angle:** real, but weaker and more structurally ambiguous
than the dock or fire-marshal angles. Some of it (Javits Public Safety,
facility damage inspection, MTCC's loading-area pass) is unambiguously
venue-direct. Some of what looks like a venue-security interaction from the
exhibitor's side (a guard checking a badge at a hall door) may actually be
an organizer-contracted proxy — which, notably, still isn't a GSC
interaction, but also isn't the *venue* interaction the thesis is about. Any
follow-up pass should treat "security guard at the door" as ambiguous by
default and check the specific venue/show before counting it as a venue
touchpoint.

## 4. Safety incidents at the exhibitor-facility interface

**The brief specifically asked for a different angle than file 10's OSHA
search-tool attempt, since that returned zero results and file 10 flagged it
as possibly a broken tool rather than a real absence-finding. Searching news
coverage for named incidents instead worked, and surfaced exactly one
convention-center-specific, named, monetarily-quantified case file 10 never
found** — a real methodological result worth flagging on its own: the OSHA
search tool's zero-results finding in file 10 looks more like a broken/
deprecated tool than a genuine "nothing happens here," now that a different
search angle turned up a real incident inside a convention center on the
first attempt.

**The Rosemont / Donald E. Stephens Convention Center forklift incident,
2024.** A man named Jason Howard was seriously injured on September 13, 2024,
inside the Donald E. Stephens Convention Center (Rosemont, IL, part of the
same convention-center circuit as McCormick Place in the Chicago metro) when
a **village-operated forklift's operator's "foot became stuck between the
brake and accelerator,"** causing the forklift to strike a printing press,
which was pushed into a heavy electrical cabinet that fell onto Howard. His
2025 lawsuit alleged the forklift had "dangerous and defective brakes." The
Village of Rosemont approved a **$500,000 settlement** in 2026 ($150,000
from the village, $350,000 from its insurer) [SOURCED,
journal-topics.com/articles/rosemont-settles-with-victim-of-forklift-accident/].
**What's not confirmed:** whether Howard was an exhibitor, an exhibitor's
employee, GSC labor, or a venue contractor himself, and whether this
happened during a specific trade show's move-in/floor period rather than
some other facility operation — the source article doesn't state his role.
The presence of a "printing press" in the room is suggestive of an
active exhibit/show floor rather than an empty hall, but that's an inference,
not a stated fact. [SOURCED as to the incident and settlement; UNVERIFIED as
to whether it specifically involved an exhibitor at the exhibitor-facility
interface, versus a venue-internal accident]

**General industry base rate, not convention-center-specific.** A separate
OSHA fatality report (August 22, 2023) describes a forklift operator backing
off a loading dock at 3:30 a.m. and being killed — but the employer was
**R&L Carriers Shared Services LLC, NAICS 484122 (General Freight Trucking)**
[SOURCED, osha.gov/ords/imis/accidentsearch.accident_detail?id=159235.015]
— a generic freight terminal, not a convention center or trade show. Cited
here only as honest context on how forklift-off-the-dock fatalities happen
in the freight industry generally (a plausible mechanism at any dock,
including a marshalling yard), not as evidence of a venue-specific incident.
NIOSH's FACE program has investigated forklift fatalities broadly (13
between 1983-1999, per a stacks.cdc.gov study) [SOURCED,
stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/181253] but this session found no FACE report
specific to a convention center, exhibit hall, or trade show move-in —
consistent with file 10's earlier finding that this specific intersection is
thinly documented in public safety-incident records generally, regardless of
which search tool or angle is used.

**Net assessment:** real evidence exists that convention-center forklift
incidents happen and produce real monetary consequences (Rosemont, $500K) —
this alone is a meaningfully stronger finding than file 10 reached via the
OSHA tool. But the sample is exactly one clearly-venue-specific case found
this session, its connection to an actual exhibitor-facility interaction
(rather than a purely internal venue-operations accident) is not confirmed,
and no dock/marshalling-yard-specific fatality or injury tied to a named
convention center surfaced despite a genuinely different search strategy.
This remains a thin evidence base — real, but thin — and should not be
oversold as proving the dock is unusually dangerous relative to fire-safety
or other floor incidents, for which this session found even less
incident-level detail (no named fire-marshal-triggered-evacuation incident,
no named ESA-inspection-removal incident, etc. — the compliance-authority
angles in §2 are well-evidenced as *processes* but not as *incident
records*, which may just reflect that a working compliance gate produces
no incident to report, while a physical dock produces incidents whether or
not the gate worked).

## 5. Complaint escalation past the GSC to venue management

**This is the weakest angle of the six by a wide margin — and the weakness
itself is a finding, not just a gap.** Multiple search angles (direct
complaint-escalation language, exhibitor-forum/blog "went over their head"
anecdotes, dispute-resolution process pages) turned up almost nothing
describing an individual exhibitor, in the moment, escalating a GSC/decorator
service failure to venue management instead of the GSC or organizer.

**What was found instead, and why it doesn't fill the gap:**
- **The Exhibitor Advocate**, described in search results as *"the only
  nonprofit association wholly dedicated to trade show exhibitor success,"*
  filling a void left when the Trade Show Exhibitor Association dissolved
  roughly a decade ago, runs a "Get Help" application process for exhibitors
  with disputes [SOURCED as to existence and framing, via search synthesis —
  the org's own "get-help" page 404'd on direct fetch this session, so its
  actual process is UNVERIFIED]. Structurally, this is a **third-party
  advocacy body**, not a direct exhibitor-to-venue channel — if it works the
  way trade associations typically do, it lobbies or mediates, it doesn't
  put an individual exhibitor in a room with venue management in real time.
- **The McCormick Place/MPEA electrical-exclusivity reform (2011)** is real,
  sourced, and genuinely resulted in venue-authority-level change: after
  sustained complaints about *"the high price, inefficient labor and poor
  customer service"* of the exclusive electrical contractor (documented via
  the American Meat Institute's own account — a 20% cost reduction and zero
  complaints reported after the reform), the **Metropolitan Pier and
  Exposition Authority (MPEA)** — the public authority that owns McCormick
  Place — opened electrical services to three pre-qualified contractors
  (GES, Freeman, Edlen) instead of one mandatory exclusive vendor [SOURCED,
  exhibitcitynews.com/reforms-spark-big-changes-at-mccormick-place-3398/].
  **This is a real instance of exhibitor dissatisfaction reaching venue-
  authority decision-makers and changing policy** — but it happened through
  **collective, trade-association-level pressure over years**, not an
  individual exhibitor's real-time complaint escalation. The article
  contains no description of an individual exhibitor walking a service
  failure up to MPEA management on the spot.

**What this suggests, tentatively:** the evidence is consistent with — though
does not prove — a structural picture where day-to-day GSC service failures
get absorbed by the GSC's *own* exhibitor-services desk (already documented
in file 09 §1/§3: order research, invoice correction, complaint resolution
handled by GSC staff specifically "to ensure customer retention"), and only
*systemic, chronic, industry-wide* dissatisfaction eventually reaches the
venue level, mediated by trade associations rather than individual
complaints. If that's right, **complaint escalation is not a meaningful
direct exhibitor-facility interaction point at the individual/real-time
level** — it's either absorbed by the GSC (not a venue interaction at all)
or aggregated by a trade association over a long horizon (a policy channel,
not an interaction). [SPECULATIVE synthesis — the absence of evidence here
is doing a lot of the work, and this session's search budget constraints
(see Honest gaps) mean this angle got less depth than the other five; a
follow-up pass specifically targeting venue GM/ops-director LinkedIn posts,
Reddit r/tradeshows-style forums, or EDPA/ESCA member complaint-process
pages might still surface individual-level evidence this pass missed.]

## 6. Venue-exclusive utility/rigging installs — the in-person technician moment

**Real and sourced at two independent venues, though this session did not
find a granular first-hand "waiting on the technician" anecdote — that
specific texture remains a gap.**

**MTCC: Showtech Power & Lighting, confirmed venue-exclusive across two
independent documents that don't cite each other.** MTCC's own current fire
regulations state: *"Rigging installation affixed to any Centre structure is
an exclusive service provided by SHOWTECH Power & Lighting"* [SOURCED, MTCC
Fire Regulations for Exhibitors 2024-25, §"Ceiling suspended items"]. An
unrelated, independently-produced organizer manual for a different show at
the same venue lists Showtech as the named electrical/lighting/banner-
hanging contact and states *"All banners MUST be hung through exclusive
supplier, Showtech Power & Lighting"* [SOURCED, TCC25 Operating Guidelines
§1 contacts and §9]. Two documents produced by different organizations
(the venue itself, and a third-party show organizer running an event there)
independently naming the same exclusive vendor is about as solid a
triangulated confirmation of real venue-level exclusivity as this kind of
research produces. MTCC's own fire-regs document separately confirms the
general principle behind it: **"Any type of utility connection (i.e.
electrical, audio, video, water, compressed air, steam, etc.) must be
carried out by the Licensor's [i.e. the venue's] authorized personnel or
its appointees"** [SOURCED, same, §"Procedures during set-up and dismantling
of shows"] — this is close to a direct restatement of the README's own
thesis language, in the venue's own regulatory document.

**McCormick Place: Edlen Electrical Exhibition Services, one of three
MPEA-approved contractors post-2011 reform.** Edlen operates a Chicago
office specifically to service McCormick Place and Navy Pier [SOURCED,
edlen.com/offices/chicago-illinois/], and is one of the three contractors
(alongside GES and Freeman) organizers may select for electrical services
following the exclusivity reform documented in §5 [SOURCED,
exhibitcitynews.com, as above]. Edlen's own exhibitor-facing FAQ confirms
the in-person labor requirement that creates the technician-moment: *"Booth
work labor is required for any connection of an electrical apparatus in the
booth space exceeding total combined wattage of a 2000watt/20amp service"*
[SOURCED, edlenimages.edlen.com FAQ PDF] — i.e. above a fairly low wattage
threshold, an Edlen electrician, not the exhibitor, must make the physical
connection.

**Javits:** the electrical function is performed by union electricians who
*"supply power lines to your booth, connecting equipment to outlets and
installing signs, booth lighting"* [INDUSTRY-COMMON/weakly SOURCED — via
search-synthesis snippets rather than a directly-read primary document this
session; consistent with, but not as strongly evidenced as, the MTCC/
McCormick Place findings above].

**What this confirms about the thesis, precisely:** these are real, direct,
in-person exhibitor-technician interactions, and they happen specifically
*because* electrical/rigging is venue-exclusive (or, post-2011 at McCormick
Place, venue-*curated* among a short approved list) — exactly the category
the README says the dock uniquely escapes. That's consistent with the
thesis's literal claim (dock ≠ exclusivity-contracted), but it also shows
that "locked to an exclusive contractor" does **not** mean "no direct human
contact" — the opposite, if anything: exclusivity concentrates the direct
technician-exhibitor interaction into a single named vendor's employees
(Showtech's riggers, Edlen's electricians) rather than removing it. **Gap**:
no first-hand account of what it's actually like to wait for that
technician (queue length, no-show rate, how exhibitors resolve a
technician not showing before a deadline) surfaced this session — this is
the one angle where the research produced confirmed *structure* but no
confirmed *friction texture*, unlike the dock (25-30 min waits at Javits,
explicitly sourced) or fire marshal (the 45-minutes-before-doors anecdote).

## 8. Does the evidence support Marshal's thesis?

**Direct answer: no, not the strong version of it — but a narrower, more
precise version survives, and is arguably a better foundation than the
broad claim.**

**The strong claim — "the dock is the ONLY part of the floor no
service-exclusivity contract covers," read as "the only unmediated
exhibitor-facility interaction point" — is not supported.** This session
found at least two other interaction points that are equally direct (no
GSC or organizer standing between the exhibitor and the other party) and,
by at least one measure, higher-stakes:

- **Fire marshal/AHJ inspection (§2)** is the cleanest counter-example: an
  external government inspector, never a venue, GSC, or organizer employee,
  physically walks the floor before every show opens, at every venue this
  session checked, with **no-recourse power to prohibit a booth from
  exhibiting entirely** — a more severe consequence than anything documented
  for a dock delay. The evidence for this is not weaker than the dock
  evidence; if anything it converged faster (three independent documents,
  immediately consistent, plus a real anecdote of the authority being
  exercised 45 minutes before doors).
- **The venue-exclusive utility technician (§6)** is a second, structurally
  interesting counter-example precisely *because* it's exclusivity-
  contracted: the exhibitor still deals with that technician in person, at
  the booth, waiting on them to make a connection before the booth can be
  considered built. Exclusivity concentrates the direct human contact into
  one vendor's staff; it doesn't remove the contact.
- **Security (§3)** is a genuine direct touchpoint at some venues (Javits)
  but the research surfaced an honest complication the brief didn't
  anticipate: at organizer-run shows, the guard at the door is sometimes
  the *organizer's* contracted vendor, not the venue's own staff — which
  means "direct interaction with a uniformed authority figure" and "direct
  interaction with the venue" are not the same claim, and conflating them
  would overstate the map.

**What does survive, and is arguably the more useful claim for Marshal to
actually build on:** among the direct exhibitor-facility interaction points
found this session, **the dock is the only one that is a recurring, scarce-
capacity, real-time SCHEDULING/ALLOCATION problem, rather than a one-time
compliance gate, inspection event, or exclusivity-vendor labor task.** Fire
marshal inspection happens once (plus rare re-inspections); ESA inspection
is the same shape; the utility technician is a one-time task per booth, not
a queue with contention across many simultaneous trucks/booths; security
checkpoints are a continuous but low-variance gate, not an allocation
problem. The dock, by contrast, is the one interaction point in this whole
map that is *structurally* a matching/queueing problem — many trucks, few
doors, a hard time window, real financial cost to idling — which is exactly
the shape of problem dock-scheduling software (Opendock, C3, Transporeon,
and per file 07's own finding, **Voyage Control, already live at MTCC,
Vancouver, and Calgary**) is built to solve. Nobody in this research found,
or would expect to find, a "FireMarshalControl" SaaS product, because a
one-time inspection with no queue isn't a scheduling problem — but a real,
funded, live competitor exists specifically for the dock, because the dock
is.

**So the honest, complicating conclusion:** Marshal's README overstates the
claim if read literally as "only unmediated interaction point" — that's
false, and this task was asked to test it honestly rather than confirm it,
so it should be corrected rather than left standing. But the underlying
commercial instinct — that the dock is worth building for, specifically,
among all these direct-interaction points — survives this test on a
narrower and more defensible ground: it's the one direct exhibitor-facility
touchpoint that is *also* a scarce-resource allocation problem with real
software-shaped structure, which is a *better* reason to build a scheduling
product there than "nobody else touches it" would have been on its own. The
"no exclusivity contract covers it" framing should probably be revised in
`01_product/01_what_we_are_building.md` to something like: *"the dock is the
one recurring physical bottleneck on the floor that is neither
exclusivity-contracted to a private vendor nor a one-time compliance
event — it's the only unmediated touchpoint that is also a scheduling
problem."* That is a more precise, more defensible, and — per file 07's
Voyage Control finding — more falsifiable claim than the one currently in
the README, and it still points at the same wedge.
