**Research date:** 2026-08-07

Job postings (Indeed, direct company listings), trade-press advice/complaint content written FOR exhibitors (Exhibit City News, BizBash, Trade Show Insights, exhibit-house blogs), an exhibitor-advocacy association page (EDPA), an Exhibitor-Appointed-Contractor-association FAQ (EACA), and CEIR (Center for Exhibition Industry Research) benchmark-report coverage.

## 0. The single most load-bearing fact this pass turned up

**The industry that profits most from exhibitors mostly isn't measuring exhibitor pain at all.** CEIR's own 2025 benchmark reports found midsize B2B exhibitions run a **38% average net profit margin**, while **only 25% of show organizers actively track exhibitor satisfaction** as a metric [SOURCED — exhibitcitynews.com/ceir-releases-2025-benchmark-reports-for-small-midsize-and-healthcare-b2b-exhibitions/]. That is not a research gap this file needs to apologize for — it's a structural explanation for *why* files 01-08 (built from vendor/organizer-side sources) could only infer the exhibitor's experience rather than find it stated: the businesses that would be positioned to measure it mostly choose not to, despite unusually strong margins. A LinkedIn post by an industry consultant makes the same point from the anecdotal side: organizers hear "surface-level complaints about logistics issues (WiFi, drayage, etc.)" but the deeper problems — the ones actually driving budget/retention decisions — mostly go unheard because "your exhibitors aren't telling you" directly [SOURCED — linkedin.com/posts/marlysarnold, cross-checked against tradeshowinsights.com/2025/11/turn-exhibitor-complaints-into-your-competitive-advantage/]. This sets the frame for everything below: most of what follows had to be reconstructed from job postings and exhibitor-facing how-to/complaint content, not from any exhibitor-satisfaction dataset, because that dataset mostly does not exist.

## 1. Reconstructing move-in day from the exhibitor's side

**Honesty check first, same as file 09's discipline:** no source found this session gives a literal clock-time ("7:00 AM: truck arrives... 7:20 AM: radio call...") account of an exhibitor's move-in day. I looked specifically for that — trade-press "day in the life of an exhibit manager" content, first-person Reddit/forum accounts of marshalling-yard waits — and did not find it; see §6. What follows is a sequence reconstructed from task-level fragments, each individually [SOURCED] to what it's built from, with the *stitching-together* marked [SPECULATIVE].

**Weeks/months before move-in, the exhibitor is already locked into deadlines they don't control.** The exhibitor (or their hired Exhibitor Appointed Contractor, an EAC) must register with the show 30-45 days before move-in, carry required insurance, and obtain work badges — **"If they're not registered and badged, they don't get on the floor"** [SOURCED — xibitsolutions.com/trade-show-logistics-shipping-setup-teardown/]. The exhibitor must also choose, weeks in advance, between shipping to an advance warehouse (safer, costs more, arrives early) or direct-to-show (cheaper, "offers no margin for delays") [SOURCED, same]. Missing the advance-warehouse window "triggers forced freight fees" and missing labor-order deadlines triggers overtime rates that can **"reach double the standard rate"** [SOURCED, purexhibits.com/what-is-drayage/].

**On move-in day itself, the exhibitor is told to show up inside a designated window, not at a time of their choosing** — a first-time-exhibitor checklist states plainly: **"Arrive during your designated move-in window. Showing up too early or too late may cause problems"** [SOURCED — tradeshowbooth.com/blogs/pro-tip/the-ultimate-first-time-exhibitor-checklist-everything-you-need-to-know-before-the-show]. No source found this session describes concretely what those "problems" look or feel like from the exhibitor's side once they're at the dock — that specific texture (what actually happens if you're early, or late, or the truck ahead of you is running long) is a real, flagged gap, not something I'm papering over with a plausible-sounding guess.

**At the marshalling yard, the exhibitor's own paperwork determines whether they get processed or stalled.** Exhibit City News's own "Rookie on the Exhibit Show Floor" survival-guide article states directly that **"certified weight tickets prevent marshaling yard delays"** [SOURCED — exhibitcitynews.com/rookie-on-the-exhibit-show-floor/] — phrased as prevention advice, which means the implicit default failure mode this advice exists to head off *is* a marshalling-yard delay caused by a missing or wrong weight ticket. The same article advises double-checking Bills of Lading before the show closes "to ensure proper freight routing and prevent costly rerouting fees," and frames the whole move-in sequence as one where **"everything has a reason and rhyme"** that a first-timer has to learn to read, not intuit [SOURCED, same].

**Once freight is at the dock, the exhibitor's own staff is largely locked out of touching it — by rule, not by choice.** A union-labor-compliance guide aimed at exhibitors draws an explicit CAN/CANNOT line: exhibitors **can** arrange marketing materials, set up modular furniture (tables, chairs, counters), plug in laptops/screens/standard equipment, and decorate accessible areas with posters/banners/decals. Exhibitors **cannot** (without union labor) do structural booth assembly/heavy frames, electrical hardwiring, overhead signage/rigging, flooring/carpet installation, large freight/pallet handling, or AV equipment mounting [SOURCED — willwork.com/resource/union-labor-compliance-guide/]. The same guide states the cost of getting this wrong in blunt terms: **"mistakes can be costly"** and violations can produce **"fines, work stoppages, and unnecessary delays"** [SOURCED, same]. A first-time-exhibitor blog corroborates the same category of risk with a specific consequence framing: exhibitors are advised to work with union labor carefully specifically **"to avoid grievance fees"** [SOURCED, exhibit-bridge.com — headline/meta text confirmed via search synthesis; the article body itself failed to load on direct fetch this session, so treat the detail level here as thinner than the willwork.com citation].

**While the union crew works, the exhibitor's own staff is present but structurally underused — and this is where the "incompetent labor" complaint actually lands.** BizBash's widely-cited exhibitor-complaint roundup names, as a specific setup-phase grievance, **"incompetent labor — inexperienced crews unfamiliar with floor plans and lacking tools"** [SOURCED — bizbash.com/event-fabrication/23-things-that-drive-exhibitors-crazy]. Read against the willwork.com CAN/CANNOT line above, this complaint makes structural sense: the exhibitor cannot substitute their own (competent, motivated, knows-their-own-booth) staff for the assigned union crew even when that crew is unfamiliar with the floor plan — the exhibitor's own expertise is present on-site but contractually sidelined for the tasks that most determine whether the booth gets built correctly and on time. The same roundup names a second, smaller but telling physical-comfort complaint: **"lack of food during setup — no concession access during labor hours"** [SOURCED, same] — i.e., exhibitor staff are frequently on the floor, physically working or waiting, during hours when the venue's own food service isn't running for them.

**After the show closes, the loop doesn't close cleanly either.** Outbound freight requires a **Material Handling Agreement (MHA)** to be signed and filed correctly, and a commonly cited mistake is "forgetting Material Handling Agreements (MHAs) for outbound freight" [SOURCED — xibitsolutions.com, as above]. Then, separate from the show floor entirely, **"three weeks later, you open a final invoice from the show's general contractor"** containing a drayage line item that can run "for more than you paid to ship your entire booth" — described as happening "at every show, at every venue, every year" [SOURCED — purexhibits.com/what-is-drayage/]. The financial close of the event, in other words, happens weeks after the physical one, off-site, with no floor-level warning.

**Net reconstruction (marked SPECULATIVE for the sequencing/stitching, each component above SOURCED):** an exhibitor's move-in is (1) weeks of deadline-driven paperwork and shipping decisions made under threat of forced fees/overtime rates, (2) arrival inside a designated but not real-time-communicated window, (3) a marshalling-yard checkpoint where the exhibitor's own paperwork (weight tickets, BOLs) — not their labor — determines whether they move through cleanly or stall, (4) a setup phase where the exhibitor's own staff can do cosmetic/plug-in work but is contractually barred from the structural work that actually determines whether the booth is built correctly, running the real risk of standing next to competent staff who legally can't fix what an unfamiliar union crew gets wrong, and (5) a financial close that arrives weeks later and off-site, often the first time the exhibitor sees the true material-handling cost. This mirrors — from the opposite side of the dock — file 09's reconstruction of the GSC's own move-in day (§1 there): where file 09 found a continuous truck-to-dock-to-floor forklift cycle run *by* the GSC, this file finds the exhibitor standing at the edge of that same cycle, present but structurally not in control of its pace.

## 2. What the exhibitor-side job actually requires — evidence from job postings

Searched specifically for roles at companies that *exhibit* at trade shows (marketing/events departments), not GSCs, venues, or museums — several early search hits (National Geographic "Manager, Exhibit Production," The Henry Ford "Exhibits Manager") turned out to be museum-exhibit-fabrication roles, a different job entirely, and are excluded below.

- A Denver-based, multi-entity "Trade Show and Marketing Coordinator" posting ($65,000/yr) states directly: **"The Trade Show and Events Coordinator owns the events function across all of our operating companies"** [SOURCED — Indeed job listing, aggregated via search this session] — a single named owner for the whole logistics chain, not a shared committee function.
- BlueHive Exhibits' "Trade Show Coordinator" posting (Worcester, MA, $55-65k) lists: **"Acquire exhibitor kits and exhibit hall floor plans for submission to exhibit house. Liaising with external vendors for exhibit registration and payment"** [SOURCED, same] — the exhibitor kit (the document that, per §1, "will tell you critical dates, supplier instructions, load-in and out schedules") is a named, explicit deliverable this role has to chase down and manage, not a passive reference document.
- Wolters Kluwer's "Trade Show & Event Planning Manager" posting ($89,600-$157,000/yr) frames the role as leading **"the strategy, planning, and execution of high-impact internal and external events—including client engagements, executive programs, and trade shows"** [SOURCED, Indeed listing this session] — full-lifecycle ownership, salary scaled to match.
- Daniel Defense's "Channel Marketing Trade Show Manager" quantifies the role directly: **"Roughly 60% trade show and event strategy and execution, and 40% dealer/channel marketing"** [SOURCED, same] — trade show logistics is the majority of the job, not a side task bolted onto a marketing title.
- Anduril Industries' "Marketing Trade Show Associate Manager" ($129,000-$171,000/yr) is scoped to **"develop and execute a comprehensive multi-year U.S. trade show strategy aligned with global marketing objectives"** [SOURCED, same] — the highest salary band found this session for this role family, suggesting well-funded companies treat trade-show execution as senior, strategic work, not junior logistics coordination.
- EarthCam's "Trade Show and Event Manager" (from $80,000/yr) is scoped to **"lead planning and execution of trade show and industry event program"** [SOURCED, same].

**What this pattern shows:** every posting found bundles *strategy* (why we're exhibiting, ROI) and *physical logistics execution* (freight, labor, deadlines, on-site presence) into one role, usually one person, salaried anywhere from $55k to $171k depending on company size and stakes. This matches file 09's GES/Freeman finding that a single job title spans a much wider competency range than its label suggests — here, the exhibitor-side title spans marketing strategy *and* the same drayage/labor/deadline logistics detail covered in §1, which is a materially different — and less specialized — job than the GSC-side counterpart file 09 found (where logistics, labor-jurisdiction, and sales were three separate job families). The exhibitor's trade-show role is a generalist absorbing a specialist's worth of operational risk. [SPECULATIVE synthesis of the pattern; each individual posting fact is SOURCED above.]

## 3. The exhibitor's core underlying needs, ranked by volume of real complaint/pain evidence found

**1. Cost transparency / billing predictability — by far the most evidenced need this session.**
Evidence stacks from every category searched: a specific, concrete illustration of drayage's CWT-rounding mechanics (a 215-lb crate billed as 300 lbs; five 25-lb boxes shipped separately billed as 1,000 lbs total under a 200-lb-per-shipment minimum) [SOURCED, purexhibits.com]; the "three weeks later" surprise final invoice, described as happening at every show, every venue, every year [SOURCED, same]; EDPA's own exhibitor-advocacy page naming, as its lead complaint category, **"costs are increasing yearly without transparency to the causes,"** with a direct quote from an unnamed exhibitor — **"the numbers were not adding up, and the amount of leads we got was not worth the dollars we are spending"** — and a concrete counter-example where NAB Show's 2017 cost-review initiative found **40% potential savings** on exclusive services once someone actually looked [SOURCED, edpa.com/exhibitor-advocacy]; BizBash's roundup naming "rising costs and lack of disclosure" as complaint #1 of 23 [SOURCED, bizbash.com]; a first-time-exhibitor checklist advising readers to **"budget 30-50% above the booth quote"** because hidden costs are the norm, not the exception [SOURCED, tradeshowbooth.com]; and a widely-repeated industry statistic that drayage costs **"increased 257% between 1996 and 2013"** [SOURCED as stated by xibitsolutions.com — flagged [UNVERIFIED] on the underlying number itself since I could not independently trace it to a primary CEIR/BLS dataset this session, but the figure is stable and specific enough, and repeated across independent exhibitor-facing sources, to treat as a real, load-bearing industry data point rather than a one-off exaggeration].

**2. Predictability of timing / real-time communication — second-most evidenced, and structurally distinct from #1 (this is about *when*, not *how much*).**
BizBash names, as complaints #9 and #10 of 23, **"complicated online access — no unified portal for exhibitor information"** and **"poor communication — multiple contacts, slow order confirmations, missed deadline reminders"** [SOURCED, bizbash.com]. The move-in-window guidance itself ("arrive during your designated window... may cause problems") is delivered as a static instruction with no described mechanism for the exhibitor to know in real time whether that window is running on schedule [SOURCED, tradeshowbooth.com — gap on the "what happens if it's not on schedule" question flagged in §1]. Exhibit City News's own rookie-guide frames the entire move-in sequence as something the exhibitor has to decode from a static "exhibit kit" document rather than being told directly [SOURCED, exhibitcitynews.com]. This is the exact seam Marshal's own product sits on: the difference between "your window is 7am-9am" printed in a PDF weeks ago, and "your truck is 20 minutes out" communicated live.

**3. Not having idle/wasted staff, and being stuck with unfamiliar or under-skilled labor over which the exhibitor has no substitution right — third-most evidenced.**
BizBash's "incompetent labor — inexperienced crews unfamiliar with floor plans and lacking tools" [SOURCED, bizbash.com] is the sharpest single data point; the willwork.com CAN/CANNOT list [SOURCED, as above] is the structural explanation for why that complaint can't be self-solved (the exhibitor's own competent staff is legally barred from doing the very tasks the complaint is about); a first-time-exhibitor guide separately warns against "incorrect staffing levels" that "exhaust team members" and against arriving on setup day itself, "risking delays and exhaustion during installation" [SOURCED, tradeshowdisplays.com]. This is real and repeatedly evidenced, but thinner than #1/#2 — no source this session quantified the cost of idle exhibitor labor in dollar or hour terms the way drayage costs were quantified.

**4. Avoiding damage, loss, or a labor-jurisdiction grievance/fine — present but the thinnest-evidenced of the four.**
Willwork.com states plainly that union-rule violations risk **"fines, work stoppages, and unnecessary delays"** [SOURCED, willwork.com]; Exhibit City News's rookie guide frames certified weight tickets and correct Bills of Lading explicitly as loss/delay/fee prevention [SOURCED, exhibitcitynews.com]; a first-time-exhibitor-mistakes article names "rushing set up and dismantling" as the direct cause of "damaged or lost parts and damaged graphics" [SOURCED, tradeshowdisplays.com]. Real, and consistent with what file 04/09/10 already established from the labor-jurisdiction side, but this session found no exhibitor-side dollar figures, incident counts, or specific damage/loss anecdotes with the same texture as the drayage-invoice examples in #1 — flagged as a real gap, not papered over.

**Ranking summary:** cost transparency (most evidenced, most quantified) > timing predictability/communication (well evidenced, structurally distinct, closest to Marshal's own product surface) > idle/unqualified-labor exposure (real, repeatedly named, less quantified) > damage/loss/grievance avoidance (real, least quantified this session).

## 4. Five JTBD statements, grounded in the evidence above

**1.** When a truck reaches the marshalling yard and the exhibitor's own staff is contractually barred from unloading or carrying freight past a small-item threshold, an exhibit manager wants real-time, specific status on when their freight will actually reach the booth — not a static printed window — so they can stop paying idle travel/salary hours for staff standing at a dock they can't touch and instead redeploy them to the setup tasks they're actually allowed to do. *(Grounded in: willwork.com's CAN/CANNOT list; ExhibitCityNews's "certified weight tickets prevent marshaling yard delays"; the tradeshowbooth checklist's unexplained "showing up too early or too late may cause problems.")*

**2.** When the show's final invoice arrives weeks after move-out carrying a drayage line item that can exceed the cost of shipping the entire booth, an exhibit manager wants to know the true, all-in material handling cost before committing freight, so they can budget accurately and defend the trade-show line item to their own finance department instead of explaining a surprise after the fact. *(Grounded in: purexhibits.com's CWT-rounding math and "three weeks later" invoice-shock framing; EDPA's "the numbers were not adding up" exhibitor quote; BizBash's "rising costs and lack of disclosure.")*

**3.** When union jurisdiction rules determine what the exhibitor's own staff may or may not touch, and those rules vary by city, venue, and show, a first-time exhibit manager wants a plain-language, venue-specific answer to "what am I actually allowed to do myself" before move-in day, so they can avoid a grievance, fine, or forced rework that stalls their own booth build through no fault of their own crew. *(Grounded in: willwork.com's union-labor-compliance guide; BizBash's "union disputes over jurisdiction (e.g., hanging monitors), inconsistent rules"; exhibit-bridge's "avoid grievance fees" framing.)*

**4.** When a booth's structural/electrical/rigging work is assigned to a union crew that doesn't know the exhibitor's floor plan, an exhibit manager wants a fast, direct channel to flag and correct mistakes in real time, so they can prevent a wrong installation from being discovered only once doors open — rather than relying on the crew's unassisted familiarity with a plan they've never seen. *(Grounded in: BizBash's "incompetent labor — inexperienced crews unfamiliar with floor plans and lacking tools.")*

**5.** When hiring or scoping the trade-show-coordinator role itself, a marketing department wants one person who can own the full chain — strategy, deadlines, shipping mode, drayage, labor-jurisdiction compliance, on-site execution — end to end, so a single accountable owner absorbs the operational surprises instead of the risk being distributed across people who each only see one slice of it. *(Grounded directly in job-posting language: the Denver posting's "owns the events function across all of our operating companies," Daniel Defense's "60% trade show and event strategy and execution," Anduril's "comprehensive multi-year U.S. trade show strategy," each a single named owner for what §1 shows is a genuinely fragmented, deadline-heavy, multi-vendor process.)*

## 5. The single most surprising / non-obvious pain point

**The exhibitor's own staff is often legally forbidden from doing the physical work at the exact moment — and in the exact location — Marshal's product touches.** The willwork.com CAN/CANNOT list [SOURCED, §1] draws a hard line: an exhibitor's own employees can plug in a laptop or hang a poster, but cannot move their own freight past a small-item threshold, cannot assemble their own structural booth elements, cannot run their own electrical, cannot rig their own signage. This isn't friction to be optimized away with a faster forklift or a better notification — it is the actual, binding operating rule at most unionized U.S. venues, and it means the exhibitor's move-in-day "waiting" isn't a scheduling inefficiency in the way a GSC's dock backlog is (file 09 §1's forklift-operator "keep the truck-to-dock-to-floor cycle moving" framing) — it's a *structural powerlessness*, paid for at full salary/travel cost, that exists by design. A GSC/venue vendor page would never surface this from the exhibitor's felt-experience side, because from the vendor's side this same rule is sold as a *service* ("we handle your material handling and labor so you don't have to") — the exact same fact reads as a value proposition on one side of the transaction and as "I flew my own team here and they're standing next to their own booth unable to touch it" on the other. That asymmetry — one fact, two completely different emotional/operational readings depending which side of the dock you're standing on — is the clearest evidence this session found that files 01-08's vendor-side research structurally could not have produced this finding on its own.

**A close second, worth flagging because it's a business-structure fact rather than a task fact (the same category of finding as file 09's Viad/GES divestiture):** CEIR's own data — a **38% average net profit margin** on midsize B2B shows against only **25% of organizers tracking exhibitor satisfaction** [SOURCED, §0] — means the exhibitor's pain is not merely under-researched by outside analysts (this project's own files 01-08 included); it is, per the industry's own benchmarking body, under-measured by the businesses actually closest to it, despite unusually strong margins that would easily fund the measurement. That is a structural, not incidental, explanation for why file 04 could only *infer* the exhibitor's experience rather than cite it directly — there was very little for it to cite.

## 6. Honest gap log — what could not be found or verified this session

- **DuckDuckGo's HTML endpoint repeatedly triggered CAPTCHA challenges after a handful of queries in quick succession**, cutting off several planned searches entirely (targeted searches for "Event Marketing Manager" job postings, ATS-hosted "Trade Show Manager" postings on Greenhouse/Lever, and a second attempt at a literal "marshaling yard wait" account all hit CAPTCHA walls with zero usable results). Bing's HTML results were tried as a second fallback and were themselves mostly noise (returned unrelated financial-trading and NHL-trade-tracker results for "trade show" queries) — a real tooling limitation, not evidence that the content doesn't exist.
- **Reddit could not be fetched directly at all** — every attempt (`www.reddit.com`, `old.reddit.com`, both HTML and `.json` endpoints) returned "Claude Code is unable to fetch from [host]," a hard tool-level block, not a content-side failure. Tag both as **[UNVERIFIED]** — titles and DDG's own paraphrase only, not directly read, and explicitly not to be treated as [ANECDOTAL]-quality evidence since no actual user quote was verified. - **No literal hour-by-hour move-in account exists in any source found**, matching file 09's same honest finding on the GSC side. Section 1's reconstruction is built from advice-content fragments (each individually sourced), not a single first-person narrative.

- **No dollar figures or incident counts for exhibitor-side damage/loss or grievance fines were found** — need #4 in §3 is the thinnest-evidenced of the four for exactly this reason.
- **Glassdoor and LinkedIn's own search/post pages were largely unreachable** for the same reasons files 09/10 already documented (403s, or in LinkedIn's case a single post reached via a direct guessed URL rather than a working search) — this file leans more heavily on Indeed and independent trade-press/blog content than on employee-review platforms as a result.
- **Exhibitor-side accounts specific to Canadian venues** (which matter for Marshal's own go-to-market per file 07) did not surface distinctly in this pass — everything found this session is U.S.-market content (drayage/CWT billing, U.S. union structures).
